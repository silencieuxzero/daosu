The bronze vessels of the Shang and Zhou dynasties carry two families of ornament, which happen to translate into a theme system.

## Cloud-Thunder Patterns: The Language of the Ground

Cloud-thunder patterns are the most basic ground pattern on bronzes, built from continuous spirals. Angular spirals are called "thunder patterns", rounded ones "cloud patterns" — together, "cloud-thunder".

They never steal the show, but they are everywhere. Just like the spirals beneath this theme's pages:

```css
--texture-body: url("data:image/svg+xml;utf8,<svg ...>...</svg>");
```

Slate on slate — faintly visible, never disturbing the reading. **A good ground pattern should be like the matrix of a bronze: it supports everything without announcing itself.**

## Animal Masks: The Presence That Guards

The animal mask, formerly known as the *taotie*, is the most striking decoration on bronzes. Symmetrical eyes, a wide fanged mouth, staring straight at the viewer.

At the footer I placed a minimal line version: horns, brows, eyes, nose bridge, mouth. All built from symmetrical straight and curved lines — flat design and Pre-Qin art agree here:

> Symmetry is not conservatism; it is a sense of order. Animal masks have used it for millennia — we only changed the way of drawing.

## Inscriptions: The Self-Awareness of the Watermark

Bronze inscriptions were cast on the inner walls of vessels, not meant to be seen — yet they became the most quiet presence. The vertical seal character in the theme header follows this idea: it does not compete with content, yet gives the page a foundation "as if cast".

## An Archaeology of a Theme

If this theme were taken apart, its layers are like strata:

1. Deepest: cloud-thunder ground (page background)
2. Second: spiral band (section dividers)
3. Third: content (text and images)
4. Topmost: animal mask and inscription (footer and headers)

Bronzes were cast in molds — a layer of clay, a layer of pattern, a layer of fire. Web pages are much the same, except the clay has become CSS.
